Introduction

The World Happiness Report (WHR) is published by the University of Oxford's Wellbeing Research Centre in partnership with Gallup and the United Nations Sustainable Development Solutions Network. It measures happiness using the following question:

"Please imagine a ladder with steps numbered from 0 at the bottom to 10 at the top. The top of the ladder represents the best possible life for you and the bottom of the ladder represents the worst possible life for you. On which step of the ladder would you say you personally feel you stand at this time?"

The WHR team has been measuring happiness for 20 years (2006-2025), and has found that six variables account for more than three-quarters of the variation in how people answer this question:

  • Having someone to count on for help (social support)
  • Income (gross domestic product (GDP) per capita)
  • Healthy life expectancy
  • Freedom to make life choices
  • Generosity towards others
  • Freedom from corruption in government or business

In the WHR data, these variables are correlated with higher levels of happiness. However, the WHR data cannot prove causality.

Section 1

How happy is the world?

Our first visualization is a baseline choropleth map of global happiness, starting in 2006. We wanted to begin with the simplest question: where are countries happier?

Color encodes the happiness score, where greener countries score higher and redder countries score lower. This gives a quick global overview and makes geographic patterns easier to notice. Instead of scanning a table of scores, you can immediately see regional clusters. Some regions run consistently higher or lower in happiness, which leads into the next question of why those differences might exist.

At this stage, the map is mostly descriptive. It does not explain the causes yet, but it gives us a starting point for asking better questions.

Section 2

What actually drives a country's happiness?

Six factors explain roughly three-quarters of the variation in happiness across countries, though they do not pull equally. The chart below tracks happiness over time, starting with the three happiest and three least happy countries.

The pattern is consistent with social support, healthy life expectancy, and GDP per capita doing most of the work, while government effectiveness and the remaining factors add smaller, steadier amounts. Wealthy Nordic countries and struggling states differ less in any single driver than in how those drivers stack up together.

How the splits are calculated: we fit a regression of happiness on these factors across every country and year. Each driver's share is its estimated effect multiplied by how far a country sits above the world's lowest value, shown as a percent of that country's score.

Section 3

How have happiness and social support moved together over time?

In the previous section, the factors that came up repeatedly were social support, healthy life expectancy, and GDP per capita. Out of these 3 factors, social support has the highest association with happiness. Three countries are shown by default to illustrate the relationship: Malawi (low social support), Costa Rica (high social support), and Serbia (rising social support). You can also add 2 other countries to see how well the relationship holds.

The graph on the left shows the relationship between happiness and social support over time with a regression line fit to all countries. Bubbles turn gray if there is no data for that year and data from the previous year is carried forward. On the right, a slope graph shows the delta in happiness for a country from 2006 to 2025.

Section 4

Social support matters but who is benefitting the most?

Let's continue to explore Malawi, Costa Rica, and Serbia to see if happiness is consistent across different age groups and genders. In addition to each demographic group, the overall happiness score for the country is provided. Annotations are placed on years where an influential event took place to also acknowledge that different countries face different realities during the same periods of time.

You change the country using the selector in the top right. The dashed line shows the country's overall happiness score for reference.

Section 5

Which countries are more or less happy than predicted?

The WHR team has found that six factors are closely associated with happiness: social support, income, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and low corruption. In the map below, we use these factors to estimate a predicted happiness score for each country. You'll see that this map looks very different from the map above!

Countries in green score above their estimate while pink countries score below it. Use the sliders to select years; the map averages each country's predicted happiness across the selected time period. Countries that are grey (and have no pop-up when you hover over them) did not have sufficient data to calculate their predicted happiness levels.

Section 6

What could explain why countries are more or less happy than predicted?

The chart below starts with the top five countries that are more -- or less -- happy than predicted. Use the sliders to choose years, then click a country bar to explore what may help explain the excess/shortage of happiness in that country.

If you click a country bar, the lower chart populates with survey data from the Gallup World Poll. For countries with positive deviance (in green), it shows the five most common survey responses where more people gave an affirmative response as compared to income-group peers. For a country that is less happy than predicted (in pink), it shows where fewer people give the affirmative response.

While these comparisons do not prove that the highlighted factors caused greater or lesser happiness in the selected country, they suggest factors that may make a difference.
